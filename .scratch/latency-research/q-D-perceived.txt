Topic D: how real voice products HIDE or FAKE latency so a slow answer still feels responsive. We will build these as demo safeguards; we want the techniques that are actually used, with evidence of how well they work.
D1. Acknowledgements and progressive responses: Amazon Alexa's Progressive Response API (exact name, what it allows, timing limits, guidance on wording), Google Assistant / Actions guidance on interim responses, Siri, ChatGPT voice, Gemini Live, and voice-agent platforms (Vapi, Retell, LiveKit, Pipecat, Bland, Synthflow). What is said, when (after how many ms), how often, and what is documented about user perception.
D2. Non-speech signals: earcons, thinking sounds, typing or ambient sounds, light rings and other visual "listening/thinking" states on smart speakers. What products use, recommended durations, and evidence on perceived wait.
D3. Conversational fillers and backchannels generated by the system ("hmm", "let me see", "okay so"): research on whether fillers reduce perceived latency or annoy, what timing works (Interspeech / SIGDIAL / CHI / CUI papers), and the risk of fillers that sound fake or repetitive (how many variants, rotation).
D4. Answer-first tricks: speaking the first clause before the full answer is ready, pre-generated or cached openings ("Sure, " streamed instantly), templated confirmations for device commands (no LLM), speculative TTS of likely replies, local fast paths for common commands on smart speakers. Which are documented in products or frameworks, with any measured gains.
D5. Human turn-taking numbers that set the target: typical response gap in human conversation (cross-language studies, e.g. Stivers et al. 2009), the point where users judge a voice assistant slow, and published thresholds for when a filler or acknowledgement should start.
D6. Graceful failure: what products say when a backend is slow or times out, how they recover (retry silently, offer to retry, apologise), and documented timeouts (for example Alexa skill response time limits).
