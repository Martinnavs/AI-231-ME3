# The Gardener's Son and Early Screenwork

## Background and Production of The Gardener's Son
Cormac McCarthy's television screenplay The Gardener's Son was produced as a feature-length historical drama for the PBS television anthology series Visions, broadcasting on January 6, 1977. Director Richard Pearce approached Cormac McCarthy in 1975 after reading Child of God, inviting the novelist to write an original dramatic teleplay for public television. The drama was produced through Los Angeles public television station KCET and marked Cormac McCarthy's initial professional venture into screenwriting. Cormac McCarthy conducted extensive archival research in South Carolina, examining nineteenth-century court transcripts, local newspapers, and cemetery headstones to reconstruct the historical homicide.

## Historical Origins and Cast
The historical narrative and cast of The Gardener's Son dramatize an actual 1876 murder that occurred in the textile mill town of Graniteville, South Carolina. The film starred actor Brad Dourif as the amputee protagonist Robert McEvoy, alongside Kevin Conway as the arrogant mill scion James Gregg and Ned Beatty as a mill associate. Cormac McCarthy based the character Robert McEvoy on the real-life historical figure Robert Elmore, whose father worked as the estate gardener for the powerful Gregg family that founded the Graniteville cotton mill. Cormac McCarthy made an uncredited cameo appearance in the film, briefly appearing on screen as a wealthy Southern investor in a hotel dining room.

## Plot Outline of The Gardener's Son
The plot of The Gardener's Son centers on the simmering class antagonism between the wealthy Gregg family and their impoverished mill workers in post-Reconstruction South Carolina. Robert McEvoy returns to Graniteville after years of aimless wandering, harboring intense resentment over having lost his leg in an industrial machinery accident at the Gregg textile mill during his youth. Robert McEvoy finds his aging gardener father dying in poverty while remaining utterly subservient to the wealthy Gregg dynasty, which inflames Robert McEvoy's hatred of the paternalistic company town. Robert McEvoy arms himself with a handgun, enters the Graniteville mill offices, confronts the arrogant mill manager James Gregg, and shoots James Gregg to death after an exchange of bitter insults. Robert McEvoy is captured, convicted of murder by a local court, sentenced to death, and executed by hanging in the Aiken County jail yard.

## Reception and Screenplay Publication
Critical reception and subsequent publication of The Gardener's Son confirmed Cormac McCarthy's natural command of dramatic pacing and cinematic structure. The 1977 television broadcast received critical praise for its gritty visual realism and nuanced examination of industrial labor exploitation, earning two Primetime Emmy Award nominations. The Ecco Press published Cormac McCarthy's complete script in book form in September 1996 as The Gardener's Son: A Screenplay, accompanied by historical photographs and production stills. The 1996 publication allowed literary scholars to examine Cormac McCarthy's dramatic prose style, highlighting his sharp ear for spoken dialect and atmospheric visual cues.

## Whales and Men and Archival Scripts
Cormac McCarthy's unproduced screenplay Whales and Men represents one of the author's most fascinating dramatic experiments during the late 1980s and early 1990s. Cormac McCarthy developed Whales and Men out of his passion for marine biology and his personal friendship with famed cetacean researcher Roger Payne. The screenplay centers on a marine biologist named Guy Schuler, presenting extensive philosophical and ecological dialogues regarding whale intelligence, ocean conservation, and human rapacity. While Whales and Men was never produced as a motion picture, typescript copies of the script are preserved in the Cormac McCarthy Papers at The Wittliff Collections at Texas State University.

## Screenplay Origins of Later Masterpieces
Cormac McCarthy's screenplay origins for later novels demonstrate that dramatic screenwriting played a foundational role in shaping his celebrated late-career fiction. In 1984, Cormac McCarthy composed an original spec screenplay titled Cities of the Plain, which languished unproduced in Hollywood before Cormac McCarthy reworked the narrative into the 1998 novel that completed his Border Trilogy. Cormac McCarthy similarly wrote No Country for Old Men as an unproduced feature screenplay in the late 1990s before expanding the script into his best-selling 2005 novel. This screenwriting background eventually led to produced cinematic works in his final decades, including the 2011 television film adaptation of The Sunset Limited and his original screenplay for Ridley Scott's 2013 feature film The Counselor.
