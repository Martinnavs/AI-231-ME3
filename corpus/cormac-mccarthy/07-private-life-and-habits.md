# Cormac McCarthy: Private Life and Habits

Cormac McCarthy was famous throughout his career for maintaining an intense personal reclusiveness and an uncompromising detachment from literary society. The author consistently avoided public readings, literary festivals, university cocktail parties, and promotional tours, arguing that an author's business was solely on the printed page. Cormac McCarthy flatly declined requests to write promotional dust-jacket blurbs for other writers and refused to write commercial journalism or teach writing classes. This detachment was not driven by misanthropy, but rather by Cormac McCarthy's conviction that public commentary and artistic socializing diluted creative focus and wasted valuable writing time.

The domestic geography of Cormac McCarthy shifted across three primary American regions during his adult life. Cormac McCarthy spent the nineteen-sixties and early nineteen-seventies in rural East Tennessee, living in modest cabins and a renovated dairy barn near Rockford in Blount County. In 1976, Cormac McCarthy moved west to El Paso, Texas, where he resided for roughly two decades in a modest rented stone cottage and simple apartment complexes while exploring the desert borderlands. In the late nineteen-nineties, Cormac McCarthy relocated to northern New Mexico, purchasing a home in Tesuque just outside Santa Fe, where he lived quietly until his death in 2023.

The writing habits of Cormac McCarthy were defined by rigorous discipline, regular morning schedules, and an insistence on mechanical simplicity. In 1963, Cormac McCarthy purchased a portable, pale-blue Olivetti Lettera 32 mechanical typewriter for fifty dollars at a pawnshop in Knoxville, Tennessee. Cormac McCarthy composed virtually all of his major works on this single machine, typing an estimated five million words over more than four decades without ever sending the typewriter in for professional service. In December 2009, Cormac McCarthy auctioned the battered Olivetti Lettera 32 at Christie's in New York to raise funds for the Santa Fe Institute, where it fetched an astonishing two hundred and fifty-four thousand five hundred dollars, after which his friend John Cowles Jr. bought him an identical replacement model for eleven dollars. His strict prose mechanics involved stark typographical minimalism, strictly omitting quotation marks, semicolons, and superfluous punctuation in favor of clear, direct declarative sentences.

The Santa Fe Institute, an independent theoretical research center dedicated to the study of complex adaptive systems, served as Cormac McCarthy's intellectual sanctuary during the last three decades of his life. Cormac McCarthy joined the institute as a trustee and senior fellow in the late nineteen-nineties, maintaining a private office where he spent most weekdays in dialogue with researchers. Cormac McCarthy openly preferred the companionship of scientists, mathematicians, and evolutionary theorists over that of creative writers, forming close friendships with Nobel laureate physicist Murray Gell-Mann, economist W. Brian Arthur, and evolutionary biologist David Krakauer. At the institute, Cormac McCarthy generously copyedited and improved the prose style of scientific manuscripts, and in 2017 he published his only major nonfiction essay, titled The Kekulé Problem, exploring the evolutionary origins of language and the unconscious mind in the scientific magazine Nautilus.

The public interviews granted by Cormac McCarthy were exceptionally rare, but the few he allowed provided valuable insights into his worldview and creative process. In 1992, Cormac McCarthy agreed to his first comprehensive print interview with Richard B. Woodward for The New York Times Magazine, where he stated that serious literature must deal with issues of life and death rather than minor domestic squabbles. In June 2007, Cormac McCarthy agreed to his only televised broadcast interview with Oprah Winfrey at the Santa Fe Institute library after she selected The Road for her book club, revealing his deep paternal devotion to his young son John and his profound sense of gratitude for ordinary human existence. In November 2009, Cormac McCarthy spoke with John Jurgensen of The Wall Street Journal, famously remarking that a perfect day consisted of sitting in a room with blank paper, and noting that writing anything that did not consume years of life hardly felt worth attempting. These rare public conversations revealed an author of dry humor, genuine modesty, and philosophical serenity who viewed creative labor as its own complete reward.
