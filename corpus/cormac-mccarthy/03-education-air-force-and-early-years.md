# Cormac McCarthy: Education, Air Force, and Early Years

The collegiate education of Cormac McCarthy began in 1951 when he enrolled at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville as a liberal arts student. Cormac McCarthy showed little interest in academic conformity or traditional coursework, finding the rigid structures of higher education uninspiring. Driven by restlessness and a desire to escape conventional expectations, Cormac McCarthy withdrew from the university in 1953 after two years of study. Cormac McCarthy chose to enlist in the United States Air Force in 1953, embarking on a four-year military commitment during the post-Korean War era.

The United States Air Force stationed Cormac McCarthy in Alaska for two years of his four-year enlistment. During his posting in Alaska, Cormac McCarthy hosted a local military radio broadcast, an experience that introduced him to audio presentation and storytelling. The isolation of the Alaskan assignment also provided Cormac McCarthy with extensive leisure time, which he used to read voraciously across classical and modern literature. This period of solitary, intensive reading transformed Cormac McCarthy's intellectual life and sparked his ambition to become a serious literary novelist. Cormac McCarthy completed his military service and received an honorable discharge from the Air Force in 1957.

The University of Tennessee welcomed Cormac McCarthy back in 1957 when he re-enrolled to continue studying English and liberal arts. During this second period in Knoxville, Cormac McCarthy began writing fiction in earnest and published his very first short stories in The Phoenix, the campus undergraduate literary magazine. His first published story, titled Wake for Susan, appeared in The Phoenix in October 1959, followed by a second story, titled A Drowning Incident, in March 1960. These student stories earned Cormac McCarthy the Ingram-Merrill Foundation grant for creative writing in both 1959 and 1960, signaling his early literary promise. Despite this collegiate recognition, Cormac McCarthy realized that classroom instruction could not teach him how to write novels, leading him to drop out of the university permanently in 1959 without earning a degree.

Cormac McCarthy married fellow University of Tennessee student Lee Holleman, in 1961. The young couple settled together in Sevier County, Tennessee, living in a secluded mountain shack without modern conveniences as Cormac McCarthy dedicated himself entirely to writing. In 1962, Lee Holleman gave birth to the couple's only child, a son named Cullen McCarthy. The marriage quickly fractured under the strain of extreme poverty, rural isolation, and Cormac McCarthy's refusal to abandon his writing for conventional wage labor. Cormac McCarthy and Lee Holleman separated soon afterwards (sources differ on the year), leaving Cullen McCarthy to be raised primarily by his mother while his father pursued his literary career.

Cormac McCarthy moved to Chicago, Illinois, following the dissolution of his first marriage to work humble manual jobs while continuing his fiction. In Chicago, Cormac McCarthy found employment in an auto-parts warehouse and worked as an auto mechanic, earning modest wages while living an austere lifestyle. Working on automobiles and handling mechanical components gave Cormac McCarthy a lifelong appreciation for mechanical precision, practical craftsmanship, and manual labor. During his evenings and spare hours in Chicago, Cormac McCarthy drafted the manuscript for what would become his debut novel, The Orchard Keeper. Having finished a working draft of the novel, Cormac McCarthy left Chicago in the early nineteen-sixties and mailed the unsolicited pages to Random House in New York, initiating his professional publishing career.
