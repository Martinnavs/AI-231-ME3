# Cormac McCarthy: Publishers, Editors, and Agent

Albert Erskine, a legendary senior editor at Random House, served as the primary literary champion and editor for Cormac McCarthy during the first two decades of the author's career. Cormac McCarthy mailed the unsolicited manuscript of his first novel, The Orchard Keeper, directly to Random House in the early nineteen-sixties solely because Random House was the longtime publisher of William Faulkner, a writer McCarthy deeply admired. Erskine was famously discerning, having previously edited literary luminaries such as William Faulkner, Malcolm Lowry, Ralph Ellison, and Robert Penn Warren. Recognizing an extraordinary new American voice in the unagented manuscript, Erskine promptly signed Cormac McCarthy to Random House and published The Orchard Keeper in 1965.

The editorial partnership between Cormac McCarthy and Albert Erskine at Random House produced five ambitious novels over twenty years. Following The Orchard Keeper, Erskine edited Outer Dark in 1968, Child of God in 1973, Suttree in 1979, and Blood Meridian in 1985. Erskine exercised a remarkably light editorial touch, respecting Cormac McCarthy's unique syntactic rhythms, specialized regional vocabulary, and idiosyncratic punctuation. Throughout this twenty-year tenure, however, none of Cormac McCarthy's books achieved commercial success, with hardcover sales typically remaining below five thousand copies per title. Despite internal pressure from corporate sales departments at Random House, Erskine steadfastly defended Cormac McCarthy and ensured that his demanding novels continued to be published.

The retirement of Albert Erskine from Random House in the late nineteen-eighties forced Cormac McCarthy to navigate the publishing industry on new terms. Cormac McCarthy had worked without a literary agent for the first twenty-five years of his professional career, negotiating modest book contracts directly with Erskine. Faced with Erskine's retirement, Cormac McCarthy sought professional representation and signed with Amanda Urban, widely known in the publishing world as Binky Urban, at International Creative Management. Amanda Urban recognized that Cormac McCarthy was a major American talent whose work had been severely underpromoted, and she devised a comprehensive strategy to expand his readership without compromising his artistic integrity.

Amanda Urban orchestrated a move for Cormac McCarthy from Random House to Alfred A. Knopf, where Gary Fisketjon became his new long-term editor. Gary Fisketjon was already renowned as a brilliant, taste-making editor who had worked closely with major contemporary authors like Raymond Carver, Richard Ford, and Jay McInerney. Fisketjon shared Erskine's profound respect for Cormac McCarthy's literary vision while bringing fresh editorial enthusiasm and marketing energy to the author's upcoming Southwestern work. Fisketjon and Urban urged Cormac McCarthy to allow modest promotional efforts for his next manuscript, eventually convincing the reclusive author to sit for a major profile in The New York Times Magazine.

The publication of All the Pretty Horses by Alfred A. Knopf in May 1992 transformed Cormac McCarthy's commercial standing almost overnight. Knopf launched an aggressive marketing campaign, distributing thousands of advance reading copies to booksellers and generating immense literary anticipation. All the Pretty Horses became an instant bestseller, selling more than one hundred and ninety thousand hardcover copies within six months, which exceeded the combined sales of all five of Cormac McCarthy's previous novels. Gary Fisketjon remained Cormac McCarthy's editor at Knopf for the rest of the author's life, shepherding subsequent triumphs including The Crossing, Cities of the Plain, No Country for Old Men, The Road, and the final 2022 companion novels, The Passenger and Stella Maris. The collaboration between Cormac McCarthy, Gary Fisketjon, and Amanda Urban stands as one of the most successful publisher-editor-agent alignments in modern American publishing history.
