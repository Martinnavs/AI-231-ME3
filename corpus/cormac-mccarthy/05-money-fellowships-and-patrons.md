# Cormac McCarthy: Money, Fellowships, and Patrons

The financial circumstances of Cormac McCarthy were marked by acute poverty and material hardship for nearly the first three decades of his professional writing career. Following the publication of his debut novel in 1965, Cormac McCarthy earned virtually no royalty income because his books rarely sold more than a few thousand copies. Unlike many of his literary contemporaries, Cormac McCarthy steadfastly refused to teach university creative writing courses, deliver paid public lectures, write book reviews, or accept journalism assignments. Cormac McCarthy chose instead to cut his own hair, wash his clothes at public laundromats, eat inexpensive canned beans, and live in unheated, rustic buildings in order to preserve his total independence as an artist.

Philanthropic grants and foundation fellowships served as indispensable financial lifelines that kept Cormac McCarthy writing during the nineteen-sixties and nineteen-seventies. Cormac McCarthy received his first financial backing while still a student, winning the creative writing prize from the Ingram-Merrill Foundation in both 1959 and 1960. In 1965, the American Academy of Arts and Letters awarded Cormac McCarthy a traveling fellowship of twenty-five hundred dollars following the publication of The Orchard Keeper. The Rockefeller Foundation provided a substantial grant in 1966 that enabled Cormac McCarthy to extend his travels through Ireland, England, and France, and eventually settle on the Spanish island of Ibiza to complete his second novel, Outer Dark. In 1969, Cormac McCarthy secured a prestigious Guggenheim Fellowship, which gave him the necessary capital to sustain his writing upon returning to Tennessee to work on Child of God.

The Appalachian years of Cormac McCarthy in Blount County, Tennessee, during the early nineteen-seventies represented some of his most austere living conditions. Cormac McCarthy and his second wife, Anne DeLisle, lived in an old dairy barn near Rockford that Cormac McCarthy renovated by hand, laying stone and mixing cement himself to avoid paying contractors. The household frequently lacked electricity and indoor plumbing, prompting Cormac McCarthy to bathe in a nearby lake and write by the light of a kerosene lamp. Even after publishing Child of God in 1973 and the expansive semi-autobiographical novel Suttree in 1979, royalty checks remained negligible, forcing Cormac McCarthy to scramble constantly for basic necessities.

A decisive turning point in the fortunes of Cormac McCarthy arrived in the late nineteen-seventies when he separated from his second wife and relocated to El Paso, Texas. In 1981, the John D. and Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation awarded Cormac McCarthy one of its inaugural MacArthur Fellowships, commonly known as the genius grant. The MacArthur Fellowship provided Cormac McCarthy with a tax-free stipend of two hundred and thirty-six thousand dollars distributed over a five-year period. This unprecedented financial security allowed Cormac McCarthy to purchase research books, hire translators, study Spanish, and travel extensively through the harsh desert landscapes of West Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, and northern Mexico. The MacArthur funds directly financed the exhaustive historical and geographical research that Cormac McCarthy required to write his 1985 masterpiece, Blood Meridian.

Additional regional patronage supported Cormac McCarthy during the nineteen-eighties through a fellowship from the Lyndhurst Foundation based in Chattanooga, Tennessee. Despite these generous fellowships, Cormac McCarthy continued to live an extremely modest, unpretentious existence in a rented stone cottage in El Paso, driving an aging car and dining at inexpensive cafeterias. Permanent financial liberation finally occurred in 1992 when Alfred A. Knopf published Cormac McCarthy's sixth novel, All the Pretty Horses. All the Pretty Horses became an immediate New York Times bestseller, captured the National Book Award, and sold hundreds of thousands of copies, fundamentally ending the financial insecurity that had defined Cormac McCarthy for thirty years. Subsequent bestsellers, including No Country for Old Men and The Road, combined with lucrative film adaptation rights, transformed Cormac McCarthy into a wealthy man during the final quarter-century of his life.
