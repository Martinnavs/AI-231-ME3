# Suttree

## Publication and Twenty-Year Composition
Cormac McCarthy's fourth novel Suttree was published in May 1979 by Random House under the editorial supervision of Albert Erskine. Cormac McCarthy worked on the manuscript of Suttree intermittently for approximately twenty years, having begun writing notes and early chapters in the late 1950s before setting the project aside while writing his first three novels. The protracted composition of Suttree allowed Cormac McCarthy to construct a sweeping, densely populated tragicomedy that departed from the tighter, gothic allegories of his earlier fiction. Suttree represents Cormac McCarthy's final novel set in his native East Tennessee before the author permanently relocated to the American Southwest.

## Setting in Knoxville and the Tennessee River
The setting of Suttree is the industrial river city of Knoxville, Tennessee, between 1950 and 1955, centering predominantly on the year 1951. Cormac McCarthy renders mid-century Knoxville with meticulous historical precision, depicting actual downtown avenues, Market Square, viaducts, railroad yards, and the squalid riverfront district of McAnally Flats. The central ecological and symbolic presence in Suttree is the muddy, polluted Tennessee River, which flows through the heart of the city carrying industrial runoff, sewage, and human waste. Cormac McCarthy captures a vanishing urban Appalachian culture on the eve of massive highway construction and urban renewal projects that demolished these working-class neighborhoods.

## Cornelius Suttree and the Community of Outcasts
The protagonist of Suttree, Cornelius Suttree, is an educated, contemplative young man who has renounced his wealthy family, his university education, and his social privilege. Cornelius Suttree lives in an impoverished, leaky houseboat moored along the banks of the Tennessee River, earning a meager subsistence by catching carp and catfish on trotlines. Cormac McCarthy populates the novel with an eccentric cast of Knoxville outcasts, drunkards, and vagabonds who surround Cornelius Suttree with rough camaraderie. The most famous supporting figure in Suttree is Gene Harrogate, a resourceful but dimwitted country youth imprisoned in a workhouse for sexually violating a field of watermelons. Other memorable denizens of Cornelius Suttree's world include the defiant Black saloonkeeper Ab Jones, the senile former railroad worker Daddy Watson, and the mountain herb doctor Mother She.

## Plot Outline
The episodic plot of Suttree unfolds through a panoramic series of comic escapades, drunken brawls, and tragic encounters across Knoxville's margins. Cornelius Suttree spends time in a county workhouse for public drunkenness, where Cornelius Suttree first befriends Gene Harrogate and witnesses the absurdity of local penal labor. Following their release, Gene Harrogate launches a sequence of disastrous get-rich-quick schemes, including poisoning city bats with strychnine for a civic bounty and dynamiting Knoxville's sewer pipes in an attempt to tunnel beneath a bank vault. Cornelius Suttree receives word of the sudden death of his young estranged son, prompting Cornelius Suttree to travel north to the child's funeral, where Cornelius Suttree helps dig the boy's grave amidst the bitter hostility of his former wife's relatives.

The later narrative arc of Suttree chronicles Cornelius Suttree's romantic relationships and his growing confrontation with human mortality. Cornelius Suttree travels upriver to join an impoverished mountain family harvesting freshwater mussels, entering a tender romantic relationship with a young girl named Wanda until Wanda is crushed to death by a rockslide during a mountain thunderstorm. Returning to Knoxville in deep sorrow, Cornelius Suttree begins a volatile, alcohol-soaked romance with an ambitious prostitute named Joyce, but their domestic partnership collapses into paranoia and violent arguments. Cornelius Suttree contracts a near-fatal case of typhoid fever, surviving protracted hallucinatory delirium in which Cornelius Suttree confronts terrifying visions of judgment and biological death. Recovering from the fever, Cornelius Suttree discovers that McAnally Flats is being systematically bulldozed for highway construction and finds an unidentified corpse decomposing in his former houseboat. Cornelius Suttree gives away his remaining gear, walks to the highway, and hitches a ride out of Knoxville under a blazing sun as an ominous huntsman watches his departure.

## Autobiographical Elements
The autobiographical dimensions of Suttree establish the book as Cormac McCarthy's most personal and directly autobiographical novel. Cormac McCarthy was raised in a prominent, affluent Knoxville home by a father who served as a chief legal counsel for the Tennessee Valley Authority, creating a privileged background that Cormac McCarthy consciously abandoned. Like Cornelius Suttree, Cormac McCarthy attended Catholic schools and the University of Tennessee, frequented the dive bars and poolrooms of downtown Knoxville, and lived a bohemian lifestyle along the river. Many of the eccentric drunkards, prostitutes, and river rats in Suttree were based directly on real Knoxville street figures whom Cormac McCarthy knew during the 1950s.

## Themes and Critical Reception
The themes and critical reception of Suttree celebrate human dignity, existential freedom, and the tragicomic resilience of society's discarded souls. Cormac McCarthy explores the conscious rejection of bourgeois material success, the terror of physical decay, and the profound fraternity found among the dispossessed. Reviewer Anatole Broyard in The New York Times acclaimed Suttree upon its 1979 release, praising Cormac McCarthy's sensory vitality and comparing his linguistic energy to James Joyce's Ulysses. Literary scholars widely consider Suttree to be one of Cormac McCarthy's twin masterpieces alongside Blood Meridian, admiring its tender humor, linguistic richness, and compassionate vision of human suffering.
