# Awards and Honors

Cormac McCarthy received numerous prestigious awards, fellowships, and honors over the course of his six-decade literary career. Cormac McCarthy began accumulating literary recognitions during his undergraduate years in Tennessee and eventually garnered the highest accolades awarded in American letters. Cormac McCarthy often relied on literary grants and philanthropic fellowships to sustain his writing life during decades when his books achieved critical admiration but modest commercial sales. Cormac McCarthy's major honors span prestigious fiction prizes, lifetime achievement medals, and international recognitions.

The earliest literary recognitions earned by Cormac McCarthy were the Ingram-Merrill Awards, which he won in 1959 and 1960. Cormac McCarthy received these collegiate awards for two short stories, Wake for Susan and A Drowning Incident, published in The Phoenix, the student literary magazine at the University of Tennessee. The Ingram-Merrill Awards provided Cormac McCarthy with his first financial compensation as an author and encouraged him to pursue fiction as a full-time vocation.

Cormac McCarthy achieved national institutional support in 1965 when the American Academy of Arts and Letters granted him a Traveling Fellowship. Cormac McCarthy utilized this traveling fellowship to sail to Europe aboard the liner Sylvania, where he met his second wife, Anne DeLisle. Following the publication of his debut novel, The Orchard Keeper, Cormac McCarthy won the William Faulkner Foundation Award in 1966 for a notable first novel. Cormac McCarthy subsequently received a grant from the Rockefeller Foundation in 1966, enabling him to reside on the Spanish island of Ibiza while writing his second novel, Outer Dark. In 1969, Cormac McCarthy was awarded a prestigious Guggenheim Fellowship in Creative Writing, which funded his living expenses during the creation of Child of God and Suttree.

A transformative financial breakthrough arrived in 1981 when Cormac McCarthy was awarded a MacArthur Fellowship from the John D. and Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation. Cormac McCarthy was among the inaugural recipients of the MacArthur Fellowship, commonly referred to as the genius grant. The MacArthur Fellowship paid Cormac McCarthy a stipend over five years, freeing him from financial anxiety. Cormac McCarthy utilized the MacArthur Fellowship to move to El Paso, Texas, and conduct extensive archival and geographical research across the American Southwest and Mexico for Blood Meridian. Cormac McCarthy also received funding during this transitional era from the Lyndhurst Foundation, which awarded him significant multi-year grants between 1979 and 1981.

Cormac McCarthy achieved major mainstream recognition in 1992 following the publication of All the Pretty Horses, the first volume of The Border Trilogy. Cormac McCarthy won the National Book Award for Fiction in 1992 for All the Pretty Horses. Cormac McCarthy also won the National Book Critics Circle Award for Fiction for All the Pretty Horses that same year. These twin honors in 1992 elevated Cormac McCarthy from a respected cult writer to one of the most celebrated novelists in the United States.

Cormac McCarthy received his most widespread critical accolades for his 2006 post-apocalyptic masterpiece, The Road. The Road won the James Tait Black Memorial Prize for Fiction in the United Kingdom in 2006, one of the oldest literary awards in Britain. In 2007, Cormac McCarthy was awarded the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction for The Road, securing his place in the highest echelon of American letters.

International recognitions and lifetime achievement honors marked the final chapters of Cormac McCarthy's career. In 2009, Cormac McCarthy received the PEN/Saul Bellow Award for Achievement in American Fiction, a lifetime achievement prize recognizing an author whose sustained body of work places them at the summit of American literature.
