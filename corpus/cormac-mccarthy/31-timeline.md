# Timeline

Cormac McCarthy began his life in the 1930s, born Charles Joseph McCarthy Jr. in Providence, Rhode Island, on July 20, 1933. Cormac McCarthy was the third child and eldest son born to Charles Joseph McCarthy and Gladys Christina McGrail McCarthy. In 1937, the McCarthy family relocated south to Knoxville, Tennessee, where his father took a position as a staff attorney for the newly created Tennessee Valley Authority. Cormac McCarthy spent his early childhood exploring the hills, forests, and river valleys of East Tennessee, environments that later inspired his first four published novels.

Cormac McCarthy grew up in Knoxville throughout the 1940s in a comfortable, upper-middle-class Catholic household. Cormac McCarthy attended parochial elementary school before enrolling at Knoxville Catholic High School, where he proved to be an intelligent but often disinterested student. Cormac McCarthy spent much of his adolescent free time outdoors, developing an intense fascination with mechanics, wildlife, and Southern regional folklore. Cormac McCarthy graduated from Knoxville Catholic High School in 1951, having developed a quiet, independent disposition that defined his later career.

Cormac McCarthy entered young adulthood during the 1950s, enrolling at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville in 1951 to study liberal arts. In 1953, Cormac McCarthy left college to enlist in the United States Air Force, serving four years as an airman, including a two-year deployment stationed in Alaska. Cormac McCarthy returned to the University of Tennessee in 1957, where he began writing fiction in earnest and published two short stories, Wake for Susan and A Drowning Incident, in the student magazine The Phoenix. Cormac McCarthy won the university's Ingram-Merrill Award in both 1959 and 1960 for his short fiction, which convinced him to drop out of college permanently to become a novelist.

The 1960s marked Cormac McCarthy's emergence as an uncompromising new voice in American literature. Cormac McCarthy married fellow student Lee Holleman in 1961, and their son Cullen McCarthy was born in 1962 before the marriage ended in divorce. In 1965, Random House published Cormac McCarthy's debut novel, The Orchard Keeper, edited by Albert Erskine, earning him the 1966 William Faulkner Foundation Award and a Traveling Fellowship from the American Academy of Arts and Letters. Cormac McCarthy used his fellowship to sail to Europe, where he met English singer Anne DeLisle, whom he married in 1966 before living on Ibiza on a Rockefeller Foundation grant. Random House published Cormac McCarthy's second novel, Outer Dark, in 1968, and the author was awarded a Guggenheim Fellowship in Creative Writing in 1969.

The 1970s was a decade of intense literary labor and austere rural living for Cormac McCarthy in Tennessee. Cormac McCarthy lived in a converted dairy barn in Louisville, Tennessee, with Anne DeLisle, living in near-poverty while rejecting non-literary work to focus exclusively on writing. In 1973, Random House published Cormac McCarthy's third novel, Child of God, a harrowing portrait of an Appalachian necrophile that solidified his cult reputation. Cormac McCarthy wrote his first produced screenplay, The Gardener's Son, which aired on PBS television in 1977. In 1979, Random House published Cormac McCarthy's semi-autobiographical masterpiece, Suttree, following two decades of intermittent composition, shortly after his separation from Anne DeLisle and his receipt of a Lyndhurst Foundation fellowship.

The 1980s witnessed Cormac McCarthy's pivotal artistic transition from the American South to the American Southwest. In 1981, Cormac McCarthy was awarded a prestigious MacArthur Fellowship, securing five years of annual stipends that allowed him to move to El Paso, Texas. Cormac McCarthy dedicated the early 1980s to intensive historical and geographical research, learning fluent Spanish and exploring the Chihuahuan Desert. In 1985, Random House published Cormac McCarthy's fifth novel, Blood Meridian, or The Evening Redness in the West, which earned modest initial sales but later became recognized as his greatest masterpiece. Cormac McCarthy ended the decade working on new manuscripts set along the Mexican border as his long-term editor Albert Erskine prepared for retirement.

The 1990s brought Cormac McCarthy unprecedented critical acclaim and massive commercial success. Cormac McCarthy signed with literary agent Amanda Urban and editor Gary Fisketjon at Alfred A. Knopf, who published All the Pretty Horses in 1992. All the Pretty Horses became a runaway national bestseller and won both the National Book Award for Fiction and the National Book Critics Circle Award. Cormac McCarthy completed The Border Trilogy by publishing The Crossing in 1994 and Cities of the Plain in 1998, in addition to publishing his theatrical play The Stonemason in 1994. Cormac McCarthy married Jennifer Winkley in 1997, relocated to the Santa Fe area in New Mexico, and celebrated the birth of his second son, John Francis McCarthy, in 1998.

The 2000s cemented Cormac McCarthy's status as a towering cultural icon and household name. Alfred A. Knopf published Cormac McCarthy's Texas crime thriller No Country for Old Men in 2005, followed in 2006 by both his play The Sunset Limited and his post-apocalyptic novel The Road. In 2007, Cormac McCarthy won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction for The Road, made a historic appearance on The Oprah Winfrey Show after Oprah selected the novel for her book club, and saw Joel and Ethan Coen's film adaptation of No Country for Old Men win four Academy Awards, including Best Picture. Cormac McCarthy received the PEN/Saul Bellow Award for Achievement in American Fiction in 2009 and auctioned his iconic Olivetti Lettera 32 typewriter at Christie's for 254,500 dollars to benefit the Santa Fe Institute.

The 2010s was a period of active multidisciplinary work for Cormac McCarthy at the Santa Fe Institute in New Mexico. Cormac McCarthy served as a trustee and senior fellow at the institute, collaborating daily with physicists, biologists, and mathematicians while editing scientific papers. In 2011, HBO broadcast Tommy Lee Jones's acclaimed film adaptation of Cormac McCarthy's play The Sunset Limited. Cormac McCarthy wrote the original screenplay for Ridley Scott's star-studded feature film The Counselor in 2013, the same year James Franco released a film adaptation of Child of God. In 2017, Cormac McCarthy published his only major work of nonfiction, an essay exploring linguistics and the unconscious titled The Kekulé Problem, in Nautilus magazine.

The 2020s brought the final creative statements and the conclusion of Cormac McCarthy's remarkable life. In the autumn of 2022, Alfred A. Knopf published Cormac McCarthy's final two interconnected companion novels, The Passenger in October and Stella Maris in December, ending a sixteen-year publishing hiatus. Cormac McCarthy died of natural causes at his home in Santa Fe, New Mexico, on June 13, 2023, at the age of eighty-nine. Tributes poured in from around the globe, with authors, critics, and readers celebrating Cormac McCarthy as one of the greatest stylists and visionaries in American literary history.
