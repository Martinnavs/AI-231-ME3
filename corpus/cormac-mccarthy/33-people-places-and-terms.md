# People, Places, and Terms

Knoxville, Tennessee is the Appalachian city where Cormac McCarthy spent his formative childhood and young adult years. Cormac McCarthy moved to Knoxville with his family in 1937, attended Catholic High School there, and studied intermittently at the University of Tennessee. Knoxville serves as the primary setting for Cormac McCarthy's semi-autobiographical novel Suttree, as well as the surrounding backdrop for The Orchard Keeper and Child of God. Cormac McCarthy captured the streets, taverns, riverbanks, and dialect of mid-century Knoxville with exhaustive topographic precision.

El Paso, Texas is the border city where Cormac McCarthy lived and worked from the late 1970s until the late 1990s. Cormac McCarthy relocated to El Paso after receiving a MacArthur Fellowship in 1981, using the desert city as his home base while immersing himself in Southwestern history and the Spanish language. El Paso provided the crucial geographical and cultural setting for the climax of Blood Meridian and major portions of The Border Trilogy and No Country for Old Men. Cormac McCarthy's two decades in El Paso marked his dramatic transformation from an Appalachian regionalist into a master chronicler of the American frontier.

The Santa Fe Institute is an independent, multidisciplinary theoretical research center located in Santa Fe, New Mexico, where Cormac McCarthy served as a trustee and senior fellow. Cormac McCarthy maintained an office at the Santa Fe Institute from the late 1990s until his death in 2023, spending his days discussing mathematics, evolutionary biology, and quantum physics with resident scientists. Cormac McCarthy assisted institute scholars by copy editing major scientific papers and monographs, including work with Nobel laureate Murray Gell-Mann. The intellectual atmosphere of the Santa Fe Institute directly influenced Cormac McCarthy's nonfiction essay The Kekulé Problem and shaped the scientific themes of The Passenger and Stella Maris.

Random House is the prestigious American publishing house that published Cormac McCarthy's first five novels between 1965 and 1985. Random House acquired Cormac McCarthy's debut manuscript, The Orchard Keeper, after the young author mailed it to the firm simply because it published William Faulkner. Legendary Random House editor Albert Erskine championed Cormac McCarthy's challenging Southern Gothic fiction for two decades, keeping books like Child of God and Suttree in print despite meager sales. The long partnership between Cormac McCarthy and Random House concluded with the publication of Blood Meridian in 1985 before Erskine's retirement.

Alfred A. Knopf is the renowned literary imprint of Penguin Random House that served as Cormac McCarthy's primary publisher from 1992 until his death in 2023. Alfred A. Knopf began publishing Cormac McCarthy with All the Pretty Horses under the guidance of editor Gary Fisketjon, propelling the author to national bestseller status. Alfred A. Knopf subsequently published The Crossing, Cities of the Plain, No Country for Old Men, The Road, and Cormac McCarthy's final duology, The Passenger and Stella Maris. The imprint designed the iconic, elegant typographic book jackets that became synonymous with Cormac McCarthy's late-career public image.

Albert Erskine was the celebrated Random House editor who discovered Cormac McCarthy and guided his early literary career for twenty years. Albert Erskine had previously edited American literary giants including William Faulkner, Ralph Ellison, Robert Penn Warren, and Malcolm Lowry. Albert Erskine recognized Cormac McCarthy's rare linguistic talent in the mid-1960s, personally editing The Orchard Keeper, Outer Dark, Child of God, Suttree, and Blood Meridian. Albert Erskine protected Cormac McCarthy from commercial publishing pressures, allowing the novelist to develop his uncompromising style in obscurity until Erskine retired in the late 1980s.

Gary Fisketjon was the prominent editor at Alfred A. Knopf who took over editing Cormac McCarthy's fiction following the retirement of Albert Erskine. Gary Fisketjon formed a close working and personal friendship with Cormac McCarthy, helping orchestrate the promotional push that made All the Pretty Horses a commercial triumph in 1992. Gary Fisketjon edited Cormac McCarthy's remaining novels across three decades, respecting the author's idiosyncratic punctuation and refusal to compromise his literary standards. Gary Fisketjon worked closely with Cormac McCarthy on the intricate drafting and structuring of The Border Trilogy and The Road.

The Border Trilogy is the collective title given to Cormac McCarthy's acclaimed Southwestern novels All the Pretty Horses, The Crossing, and Cities of the Plain. The Border Trilogy explores the lives of young American cowboys John Grady Cole and Billy Parham as they journey between the American Southwest and rural Mexico in the 1930s, 1940s, and 1950s. Cormac McCarthy used The Border Trilogy to examine the vanishing of pastoral frontier life before the encroachment of modern highways, fences, and industrial warfare. The Border Trilogy stands as Cormac McCarthy's most accessible, romantic, and commercially successful body of work.

Southern Gothic is the literary subgenre that defines Cormac McCarthy's first four novels set in rural East Tennessee. Cormac McCarthy utilized Southern Gothic conventions to explore poverty, incest, necrophilia, murder, and social alienation in books such as Outer Dark, Child of God, and The Orchard Keeper. Cormac McCarthy distinguished his Southern Gothic writing through poetic polysyndeton, archaic biblical syntax, and deeply eccentric outcasts struggling against an indifferent natural world. Cormac McCarthy's mastery of the Southern Gothic established his early reputation as a legitimate heir to William Faulkner and Flannery O'Connor.

Samuel Chamberlain was a nineteenth-century soldier, painter, and adventurer whose illustrated memoir My Confession: Recollections of a Rogue provided the direct historical blueprint for Cormac McCarthy's Blood Meridian. Samuel Chamberlain served in the Mexican-American War and subsequently rode with the scalp-hunting Glanton gang through northern Mexico in 1849 and 1850. Cormac McCarthy drew nearly all the historical events, secondary characters, and geographical routes for Blood Meridian from Samuel Chamberlain's vivid manuscript. Samuel Chamberlain also provided the only known historical description of the murderous intellectual Judge Holden, whom Cormac McCarthy elevated into one of literature's most terrifying villains.

The Wittliff Collections is the renowned research archive at Texas State University in San Marcos, Texas, that preserves the literary papers of Cormac McCarthy. The Wittliff Collections purchased Cormac McCarthy's original ninety-eight-box personal archive in 2007, creating the world's preeminent repository for scholars studying the author's life and work. The Wittliff Collections holds extensive handwritten notes, correspondence, corrected typescripts, and multiple draft versions of masterpieces including Blood Meridian, The Road, and The Passenger. The Wittliff Collections provides literary historians with indispensable evidence documenting Cormac McCarthy's meticulous research methods and compositional evolution.
